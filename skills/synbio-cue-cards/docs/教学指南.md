# Synthetic Biology Speaker and Cue Cards: Teaching Guide

This guide provides a 35-minute exercise in project reasoning and communication. It suits secondary students or university beginners who can read a short project description; no laboratory work is required. The teacher prepares the `cards-example.json` teacher version and a student version with answer-like `notes` removed.

## Learning goals and observable outcomes

By the end, students should be able to split a project claim into “available material, supported judgment, and missing evidence,” explain it in one minute, identify when a peer presents an assumption as fact, and accurately acknowledge what has not yet been verified when questioned.

## Classroom sequence

| Time | Card use | Teacher action | Student evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0–5 min | Speaking card: “First clarify the question” | Read a sample and highlight who, context, and problem; do not teach experimental procedures | Each student writes a problem statement and circles an assumption |
| 5–12 min | Student question card: “Is the evidence sufficient?” | Give one minute of silent thinking, then pair discussion; ask who is covered by the conclusion | Note one inferential leap and one needed source |
| 12–20 min | Teacher-version question card | Ask two groups for different revisions and compare their evidentiary scope; use hints only afterward | Revise a conclusion and explain the change |
| 20–30 min | Defense card | In groups of three, rotate speaker, questioner, and observer; each round: one-minute talk, one-minute follow-up, one-minute feedback | Observation sheet marking facts, assumptions, and unknowns |
| 30–35 min | Restate without cards | Use a new scenario for transfer, not memorization of the original example | 60-second spoken answer and one next-source request |

## How question cards support thinking

The student deck shows only the question. Teacher hints are prompts, not mandatory answers: Which population does your conclusion concern? Does current material cover that scope? What else could explain the result? What new source might change your judgment?

If a student only says “the sample is too small,” ask in what way and how that affects the conclusion. If they suggest collecting more material, ask how it connects to the original question. Do not use “do more experiments” as the universal answer.

## Gradually reduce reliance on speaking cards

In round one, students may read full sentences while focusing on meaning. In round two, reduce the body to four keywords—problem, evidence, limitation, next step—and generate a new deck. In round three, remove the cards and ask students to explain in their own words. The observer notes what became inaccurate without the card; add only that support for the next round.

## Assessment rubric

Score each item 0–2, for 8 points total. Show the criteria to students. Assess communication and reasoning; do not treat volume or memorization as scientific understanding.

| Dimension | 0 points | 1 point | 2 points |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Clear problem | Broad slogan only | Problem stated, but setting is unclear | Names user, setting, and difficulty |
| Evidence and claim match | Invented evidence or unsupported conclusion | Evidence exists but generalization is too broad | Claim scope matches available material |
| Uncertainty | Assumption presented as fact | Acknowledges unknowns vaguely | Names a specific unknown and needed source |
| Response to questions | Avoids the question or repeats a script | Partly answers | Answers directly, then gives evidence and limits |

Eight points is not a prediction of competition awards. If a student scores 0 on evidence, check the sources and rewrite that card before presentation practice.

## Adjust difficulty and keep records

For beginners, provide the sentence stem: “We currently know…, but this does not yet show…”. For advanced students, ask them to compare two explanations and state what information would change their judgment. Teachers can retain question cards, before/after revisions, and scores without collecting real names; use role names in shared examples.

After class, ask students to turn one overconfident statement into a checkable claim, with a real source or a clear “to be verified” label. Begin the next session with these revisions and check transfer to a new question.
