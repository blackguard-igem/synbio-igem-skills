# Synthetic Biology Planner: Teaching Guide

This guide helps teachers use AI to develop students' planning skills. Students remain responsible for asking questions, choosing evidence, making trade-offs, and explaining decisions.

## Learning goals and starting point

Beginners should distinguish “whom are we helping?”, “what is the scientific mechanism?”, and “how can the project test it?” More advanced students should compare alternatives, identify resource constraints, and revise a plan when new evidence arrives. Begin with a short task to understand prior knowledge rather than guessing from age.

Suggested cycle: students draft a one-page plan independently; AI asks a few questions about omissions; teams support choices with sources; peers review; students revise and explain one actual change. AI may model questions, but should not complete a submission-ready deliverable before students think.

## Teaching simulation: 90-minute planning exercise

Scenario: a fictional school team wants to use public sources to study “how to compare the evidence maturity of different biomanufacturing routes.” No experiments will be conducted, and no route will be claimed to outperform a real product.

| Time | Activity | Student evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–10 min | State the question, audience, and known constraints | Three-sentence brief |
| 10–30 min | Compare two candidate directions and list reasons for and against each | Comparison table and sources to verify |
| 30–50 min | Break work into tasks, role owners, and checkpoints | One-page task plan |
| 50–70 min | Peer review and identify one untested assumption | Review comment with rationale |
| 70–90 min | Revise and explain decisions | Before/after comparison and exit ticket |

## Adaptable assessment rubric

The scores below are an instructional example designed by the author. Teachers should adapt them to course goals; this is not an official iGEM scoring rubric. Score each dimension from 0–2: 0 means missing or misunderstood; 1 means partly clear but lacking support or connection; 2 means specific, traceable, and fit for the task.

| Dimension | Observable evidence for 2 points |
| --- | --- |
| Problem definition | States who is served, the scope, and what is out of scope |
| Use of evidence | Separates sourced facts, inference, and gaps; does not invent data |
| Feasibility | Tasks have role owners, dependencies, deliverables, and acceptance checks |
| Comparison and trade-offs | Explains the choice and the cost of rejecting an alternative |
| Reflection and revision | Identifies which decision changed after feedback and what remains uncertain |

Use process documents and explanations for formative assessment. A final assessment may use a revised plan and a short defense. Do not grade primarily on formatting, number of technical terms, or length of AI output.

## Differentiation and teacher involvement

For beginners, provide two or three screened materials and a partially completed template. Ask advanced students to search independently and discuss conflicting sources. Common misconceptions include treating a need as a solution, speculation as data, and completing an activity as proof of impact. Ask: “What observation would make you revise this decision?” For real experiments, personal-data collection, institutional contact, and official competition submissions, the responsible lead must determine requirements and authorization; a classroom simulation does not replace review.
